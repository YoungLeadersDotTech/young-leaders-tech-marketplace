# The method

Six techniques for learning a talk off by heart in a day or two, without speaker notes. The first
two are good memory practice for anyone. The last four are the ones that matter most when focus is
the bottleneck: mark only those four (and the timed drill) with the superscript ADHD tag, with the
legend "ADHD = tailored for ADHD, useful for all". Tagging everything makes the tag meaningless.

The method came from rehearsing a 20-slide, 15-seconds-per-slide talk in 48 hours.

## 1. Scenes, not paragraphs (untagged)

Split the talk into three or four chapters, each with one job: the problem, the turn, the results,
the takeaway. Four scenes are far easier to hold than twenty slides.

**Why it works:** chunking cuts the load on working memory. You only ever carry one scene
in your head at a time.

## 2. One cue word per slide (untagged)

Pick a single word that pulls the whole slide back. The cue is the hook and the sentences hang off
it. Prefer a concrete noun or a number from the slide over an abstract word. Cues that start with
the same word ("Trust" and "Trust breaks") are easy to mix up mid-talk: make them distinct unless one
is a deliberate callback ("Fax" and "Fax again").

**Why it works:** one word is far easier to retrieve under pressure than a sentence. When
your mind blanks, the cue comes back first.

## 3. Say it out loud, early and often (ADHD)

Reading silently feels like rehearsal but it isn't. Speak it while pacing the room, record one
natural take on your phone and listen back on a walk or while doing chores.

**Why it helps with ADHD:** movement plus audio keeps you engaged long enough to actually get the
repetitions in.

## 4. Anchor it to your body (ADHD)

Tie each chapter to a spot on the stage or a gesture: left side for the opening story, centre for
the big claim, a downward hand for "below the surface".

**Why it helps with ADHD:** a physical anchor gives memory a second way in that doesn't depend on
focus.

## 5. Change the rhythm (ADHD)

Run it at normal speed, then fast, then slow, then as if you're telling a friend over coffee. Each
pass shakes loose the bits you only know as a block.

**Why it helps with ADHD:** a bit of novelty every pass stops rehearsal going stale, so you keep
doing it.

## 6. Lock the edges (ADHD)

Learn the first and last line of every chapter word for word. The middle can flex; the transitions
can't.

**Why it helps with ADHD:** transitions are where attention drifts. Fixed edges pull you back on
track.

## The recall chain

The first cue word of each chapter, in order (for example: Fax, Evolution, Copy button, States).
Picture it before bed and again before walking on. If the speaker blanks mid-talk, they find their
place on the chain and the next cue follows.

## The plan (default: the night before and the morning of)

In the HTML coach this is the opening "Start here" tab: the next unticked step is highlighted and
each step that names a tab has a button that opens it.

**Tonight**
- Read the script out loud once while pacing the room.
- Record one full take on your phone. Natural pace, no restarts.
- Listen back once on a walk or while doing chores.
- Quiz yourself on the cue words once, then re-drill only the ones you missed.
- Before bed: picture the recall chain, in order.

**Tomorrow**
- One timed run with slides moving on automatically at the real pace.
- One slow run, exaggerating every anchor and pause.
- Read the cue cards once, then put them away.
- Final run with no notes at all. Stop rehearsing after this one.

**Why it works:** short, varied passes spread over an evening and a morning beat one long
cram. Sleep does part of the work.

With more than two days, spread the same passes across the extra days and keep the final no-notes
run for the last morning.

## Pacing

Spoken English runs at about 150 words a minute, or 2.5 words a second. A 15-second slide holds
about 35 to 40 words.

- **Auto-advance talks** (Ignite, PechaKucha, 15-second lightning talks): every slide has the same
  seconds. Flag a slide that needs more than 1.2 times its seconds and offer a trim.
- **Free-paced talks** (a time limit, slides move when you click): give each slide its own seconds,
  estimated from its words, and check the total against the limit. Flag the total, not individual
  slides: a dense demo slide is allowed to take longer than a title slide.
